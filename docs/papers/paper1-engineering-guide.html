Buy Headroom, Not Cleverness

An engineering guide to quadrotor pursuit of fast fixed-wing aircraft

Draft, 2026-11-02. Every bound quoted as "proved" or "certified" is machine-checked in Lean 4 (repository AbdullahAlmanei/vision-tracking-lean, no sorry, standard axioms only). Every number is reproducible with the scripts named in the appendix.

Abstract

A quadrotor has to keep a fast radio-controlled fixed-wing aircraft (3.5 m long, 2.5 m span, up to 55 m/s, turning down to a 350 m radius) inside the image of one camera in every frame. We give conditions that are proved sufficient for this, and conditions that are proved necessary for any strategy, even one that knows the target's future. We then sweep the design parameters through those bounds.

The results reduce to three design rules: 1. Top speed is set by thrust and air. Thrust-to-weight is the strongest lever. The proved speed wall scales as (F_max² / (k_n·c))^¼. 2. Agility is set by yaw authority when total thrust is low. The worst case is a 35 m/s dive, not a 55 m/s dash. Thrust-to-weight does not help there; yaw authority and low inertia do. 3. The camera is solved by geometry. Fly underneath, with a rear-facing top camera on a 30–70° hinge, or a fixed mount with a 120° lens.

Strategies we tested as alternatives to plain copying either lost or needed physically impossible torque: looser formations, smoothing planners, capped trackers, winged frames and weaving. For the baseline 1.5 kg vehicle the certified envelope reaches 52–53 m/s, against a proved physical wall of 57.7 m/s.

1. The answer on one page

Baseline vehicle: - 1.5 kg, thrust-to-weight 6, plus layout, 18 cm arm, yaw coefficient 0.02; - inertia (0.012, 0.012, 0.02) kg·m², parasitic drag c = 0.008 m⁻¹; - rotor-disc normal-force coefficient k_n = 0.01 m⁻¹ (about 0.02 m² of effective normal area); - rear-facing top camera on a 30–70° hinge, 120° field of view.

Baseline target: - 20 m/s up to v_max, turn radius 350 m; - along-track acceleration 2 m/s², vertical acceleration 1.5 m/s²; - snap 15 m/s⁴, jerk 1–5 m/s³, climbs and dives up to ±5° or ±10°.

Spend in this order: 1. Thrust-to-weight. About +5 m/s of wall and certified speed per unit of thrust-to-weight. 2. Rotor-disc and frame normal area (k_n). Halving it buys about 10 m/s. 3. Yaw authority, and low yaw inertia. This lifts the ±10°, jerk-5 dive case from 33 to 52 m/s. 4. Camera: a hinge, or a lens of at least 120°.

Do not spend on a longer arm, a cleaner airframe (it hurts dives), wings, or a cleverer planner.

2. How to read the numbers

Two kinds of bound: - Proved necessary (the wall). If the target's top speed exceeds it, a straight, level target escapes every drone trajectory, even one planned with full knowledge of the target's future. This is NecPower / master_necessary_power (rotor-disc model) and NecIneq3 / master_necessary3 (isotropic model). - Proved sufficient (certified). If MasterIneq3 holds, a concrete position-only strategy keeps every admissible target that starts in steady flight in view in every frame. That is master_sufficient3. The certified v_max is the largest value for which the inequality holds, found by bisection over the exact mirror of the Lean budget (scripts/budget_v3.py).

What the model includes: - a full rigid-body quadrotor in discrete time (δ = 1/240 s): four bounded motors, mixer, Euler's equation, exact rotations, tilt, body-rate and speed limits; - parasitic drag c‖v‖v, and a rotor-disc normal force −k_n‖v‖⟪v, b₃⟫ b₃ with centre of pressure at the centre of mass; - a camera on a one-axis hinge with a rate limit, and visibility meaning within detection range and inside the field of view.

What the model leaves out: - rotor thrust that depends on airspeed; - aerodynamic moments; - wind; - the ground; - sensor noise.

Read every number as "under this model". Section 9 lists what to measure to make them yours.

3. Geometry: approach from below, look back and up

3.1 Why underneath

Fast flight tilts the quadrotor hard: about 50° of pitch at 35 m/s and about 70–80° at 55 m/s. That tilt decides where a body-mounted camera can point.

  • Belly camera, looking down from above. It is capped near 30 m/s in our earlier version: at speed the thrust axis tilts so far that the target leaves the field of view.
  • Looking down from above is also a losing position on its own. A drone that sits higher than D·sin e(v) above the target (D = detection range, e = the depression angle steady flight allows) must pitch beyond its drag balance to look down. It is then pushed forward, overtakes, and loses the target. QuadTraj.forced_loss_time gives an explicit time.
  • In a prototype with a 382 m range and a 90° lens: about 17 s at 20 m/s and 300 m above; about 53 s at 30 m/s.
  • Above 40 m/s the drag balance itself provides the downward look.
  • Underneath, with a top camera. Pitching nose-down for speed rotates a rear-facing top camera up and over. Speed helps this camera.

3.2 Rear-facing, not forward-facing

On a nose-down airframe a forward-facing top camera points at elevation η − p, where p is the pitch. At speed that is at or below 0: it cannot look up. A rear-facing camera points at p + η measured from the rear horizon, so it reaches straight up at speed. Mount the camera on the rear face of a cone-shaped head and hinge it about the pitch axis.

3.3 Hinge or wide lens

With the yaw locked to the target's sideways direction, the camera's off-target angle is the bank angle plus the hinge's miss. Bank stays at or below 38° and the offset at or below 40° across the envelope. Certified v_max, isotropic model, ±5° dives, jerk 3:

Camera 120° lens 90° lens
Hinge 30–70° 69.9 m/s 69.9 m/s
Hinge 20–80° 69.9 69.9
Fixed at 50° 69.9 50.1
Fixed at 60° — 34.6

Rule: with a ≥120° lens a fixed mount near 50° is enough. With a 90° lens use a hinge of at least 30–70°. Pitch sweeps about 80° across the 20–55 m/s band, and only the hinge or the extra field of view absorbs it.

3.4 Offset and ground clearance

The formation offset (100 m below in our runs) barely matters for the bounds; 60–150 m behave alike. It does set the ground clearance: the target must fly at least the offset plus a safety margin above ground. Plan for 60–100 m.

4. Top speed: thrust and air

4.1 The physics

The air pushes on the rotor plane with acceleration −k_n‖v‖⟪v, b₃⟫ b₃. At speed the rotor disc faces the wind (about 68° of tilt at 55 m/s), so this term is first-order, as large as the thrust needed against parasitic drag.

The power the motors can put into the flight path is therefore capped at min(F_max‖v‖, F_max²/(4k_n‖v‖)). Parasitic drag takes c‖v‖³. Energy balance, together with the fact that the drone must stay within camera range of the target, gives the wall (no_traj_keeps_of_power). Its leading-order scaling is

V_wall ≈ (F_max² / (4·k_n·c))^¼.

The scaling law holds closely across the sweeps: - doubling F_max multiplies the wall by 1.42 (√2 predicted); - quadrupling k_n multiplies it by 0.71 (4^−¼ = 0.707 predicted); - c from 0.006 to 0.010 multiplies it by 0.88 (0.88 predicted).

The certified envelope tracks the wall at about 90%.

4.3 How tight the wall is

  • The 57.7 m/s wall is physical, not slack. Steady straight, level flight at full collective reaches 57.3 m/s. The 0.4 m/s difference is the energy gain of the 240 Hz Euler step.
  • Weaving does not beat steady flight on a real quadrotor. A point mass that banks left and right fast can beat steady flight: over-thrusting sideways tilts the disc off the wind and cuts its drag (47.8 vs 47.0 m/s at k_n = 0.02). On a rigid body those flips need motor thrusts of −34 to +78 N against a 22 N limit, just when the collective is already at maximum. Every weave within the limits was slower than steady flight (scripts/explore/weave.py).

4.4 Rules

  • Thrust-to-weight first. It is the only lever that raises both the wall and the certified envelope by about 5 m/s per unit.
  • Minimise the area the rotor plane presents to the wind: small discs relative to thrust, and a thin frame in the disc plane. k_n is worth measuring (§9).
  • Do not chase parasitic drag. Lower c raises the wall weakly (c^−¼) and costs agility in dives (§5.3).
  • Keep a motor-thrust reserve at top speed. The top motor saturates first: total thrust for speed plus differential thrust for steering. The certified envelope ends where that reserve runs out.

5. Agility: the 35 m/s dive

5.1 Why the middle speed is the worst

A quadrotor steers by tilting, and tilts with differential motor thrust. The weakest motor cannot go below zero, so torque authority scales with total thrust.

In a dive, gravity and drag both point along the path, so the motors need little total thrust. At 35 m/s in a 10° dive the minimum collective falls to about 7 m/s², below hover. At the same speed, a change in the target's acceleration (jerk) swings the thrust direction fastest, because the collective is small.

10° dive, base quad, jerk 5 20 m/s 35 m/s 55 m/s
Torque available per motor 2.8 N 2.4 N 7.6 N
Torque needed per motor 1.5 N 6.1 N 1.5 N

At 55 m/s drag rescues the drone: about 24 m/s² of drag means a large collective and small attitude swings.

Inertia scale 0.7 / 1.0 / 1.4 52.1 / 33.4 / 30.1

Thrust/weight 4 → 8 33.4 at every value

Yaw torque binds, not roll or pitch. The frame is locked to the target's sideways direction, so a change in the thrust axis forces a yaw acceleration about the thrust axis in proportion to the bank.

The remedies, in order: 1. a larger yaw reaction coefficient (propellers and motors with more reaction torque per newton, or canted motors); 2. lower yaw inertia (mass close to the centre); 3. a longer arm, which does nothing here because it helps roll and pitch only.

Extra thrust does not help either, because the problem is low minimum collective, not low maximum.

5.3 The drag paradox

A cleaner airframe dives worse. At c = 0.006 the ±5° jerk-5 envelope drops from 52.1 to 39.3 m/s, because less drag means even less collective in a dive. Drag is the quadrotor's air brake, and in pursuit the air brake is what keeps torque authority alive.

5.4 Wings make it worse

We prototyped a winged quadrotor: cambered arms giving lift along the camera direction, and symmetric sections on the other axis. - Lift unloads the motors. In a 35 m/s dive the minimum motor thrust falls to about 0, and the available torque falls to 0.01–0.28 N against a need of 0.52 N. - The sensitivity of bank angle to sideways acceleration stays at about 1/g (5.9° per m/s²) for every airframe.

Wings would need control surfaces, and then the vehicle is no longer a quadrotor. Do not add lifting surfaces to a pursuit quadrotor.

6. Strategy: copy exactly, from below

The certified strategy: - Planner: a sparse 3-tap filter, exact for constant acceleration, that sees target positions with a 4-frame delay at 240 Hz. - Formation: the drone flies the filtered target path offset straight down. - Attitude: the thrust axis follows the planned acceleration, and the yaw is locked to the target's sideways direction. - Camera: the hinge points straight up, clamped to its range.

We tested five alternatives. None improved on it.

Alternative What happened
Smoothing or lag-compensating linear planners Extrapolating through a 4-frame delay forces filter gain Σ|h| ≳ 2, so planned jerk and snap never drop below the target's own
Capped (jerk-limited) nonlinear tracker Against adversarial targets: lags of 39–325 m and 13–35 m/s; the target reverses just as the tracker catches up
Loose yaw, averaged formation, looser offsets Torque saved is paid back in bank, thrust margin or lag
Weaving to reduce rotor-disc drag Needs −34 to +78 N motors (§4.3)
Winged frame Motors go idle in dives (§5.4)

There is also a negative result on the proof side: no torque requirement of practical size can be forced on the drone. With 570 m of detection range and a 120° lens, a drone may respond over 7–30 s, which forces only 0.002–0.007 rad/s of body rate. The agility limits come from the tight formation, not from physics. They could only be relaxed by a formation that spends range and field of view as slack. We did not find one that pays off.

Rule: invest in hardware headroom and a clean, high-rate planner (240 Hz, small fixed delay). Do not invest in clever trajectory shaping.

7. Rules of engagement

  1. Take position below and behind, at 60–100 m, before the target reaches top speed.
  2. Expect the hardest moment at mid speed in a dive, not at top speed. If the target dives at 30–40 m/s while rolling into a turn, that is where the margin goes.
  3. Targets that escape any quadrotor: - sustained level flight above the wall (57.7 m/s for the baseline); - in the isotropic model, sinking straight down faster than the drone's own terminal fall speed √(g/c) (35 m/s at c = 0.008). No strategy recovers either.
  4. Never trail from above at more than D·sin e*(v) of height. You will be forced past the target.
  5. Planner rate is a design input. The proved budgets use 120–480 Hz with a 4-frame delay. Slower loops scale the frame-to-frame terms up.

The recommended package, verified. Thrust-to-weight 7, k_n = 0.005, yaw coefficient 0.03 and 30% less inertia give:

±5° dives ±10° dives
Proved wall 73.9 m/s 73.9 m/s
Certified, jerk ≤ 3 68.2 58.6
Certified, jerk ≤ 5 68.1 58.4

So a 55 m/s target is covered with margin in every case.

Levers interact. Without the inertia cut the ±10°/jerk-5 case drops back to 35.5 m/s. Lowering k_n also lowers the collective in dives: the drag paradox of §5.3 applies to the rotor disc too. Always re-run the sweep for the full package, not lever by lever.

Above about 59 m/s in 10° dives the 85° tilt limit binds. A higher tilt limit is the next lever for steep, fast dives.
